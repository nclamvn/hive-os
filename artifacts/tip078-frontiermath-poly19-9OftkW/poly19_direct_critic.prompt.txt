## Proof

Define polynomials by
\[
D_0(x)=2,\qquad D_1(x)=x,\qquad
D_n(x)=xD_{n-1}(x)-D_{n-2}(x)\quad(n\ge2).
\]
Take \(p=D_{19}\). The recurrence gives integer coefficients, and induction shows that \(D_n\) is monic of degree \(n\) for \(n\ge1\), with
\[
D_n(-x)=(-1)^nD_n(x).
\]
In particular, \(p\) is odd and monic.

To calculate its coefficients, the recurrence gives the formal generating function
\[
\sum_{n\ge0}D_n(x)t^n=\frac{2-xt}{1-xt+t^2}.
\]
Expanding the denominator geometrically and collecting coefficients gives, for \(n\ge1\),
\[
D_n(x)=
\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
(-1)^j
\left[
\binom{n-j}{j}+\binom{n-j-1}{j-1}
\right]x^{n-2j},
\]
where a binomial coefficient with lower index \(-1\) is zero. Thus
\[
\begin{aligned}
p(x)={}&x^{19}-19x^{17}+152x^{15}-665x^{13}
 +1729x^{11}\\
&-2717x^9+2508x^7-1254x^5+285x^3-19x.
\end{aligned}
\]
This verifies the supplied coefficients and the required linear coefficient.

We next prove an exact factorization of the equality curve. The recurrence implies the Laurent-polynomial identity
\[
D_n(u+u^{-1})=u^n+u^{-n}.
\]
Indeed, it holds for \(n=0,1\), and multiplication by \(u+u^{-1}\), followed by subtraction of the preceding term, proves the induction step.

Let
\[
\zeta=e^{2\pi i/19},\qquad
c_k=\frac{\zeta^k+\zeta^{-k}}2
=\cos\frac{2\pi k}{19}\quad(1\le k\le9).
\]
We claim
\[
p(y)-p(x)=(y-x)\prod_{k=1}^{9}Q_k(x,y),
\]
where
\[
Q_k(x,y)=x^2+y^2-2c_kxy-4(1-c_k^2).
\]

To prove this universally, take \(u\) transcendental over \(\mathbb C\), set \(x=u+u^{-1}\), and work over \(\mathbb C(u)\). The nineteen elements
\[
y_j=\zeta^j u+\zeta^{-j}u^{-1},
\qquad 0\le j\le18,
\]
are distinct: their coefficients of \(u\) are distinct. Moreover,
\[
p(y_j)=(\zeta^j u)^{19}+(\zeta^j u)^{-19}
=u^{19}+u^{-19}=p(x).
\]
Consequently the monic degree-19 polynomial \(p(y)-p(x)\) is
\[
\prod_{j=0}^{18}(y-y_j).
\]
Here \(y_0=x\). Pairing indices \(k\) and \(19-k\) gives
\[
y_k+y_{19-k}=2c_kx,\qquad
y_ky_{19-k}=x^2-4(1-c_k^2),
\]
so each paired product is \(Q_k(x,y)\). Finally, \(u+u^{-1}\) is transcendental over \(\mathbb C\): otherwise \(u\), which satisfies
\[
u^2-xu+1=0,
\]
would be algebraic over \(\mathbb C\). Thus substitution \(x=u+u^{-1}\) is injective on \(\mathbb C[x,y]\), proving the claimed polynomial identity.

Each \(Q_k\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb C\). Viewed as a monic quadratic in \(y\), its discriminant is
\[
\Delta_k(x)=4(1-c_k^2)(4-x^2).
\]
Since \(0<2\pi k/19<\pi\), we have \(c_k\ne\pm1\). Therefore \(\Delta_k\) has simple zeros at \(x=2\) and \(x=-2\). A square of a rational function has even order at every zero and pole, so \(\Delta_k\) is not a square in \(\mathbb C(x)\). The quadratic is therefore irreducible over \(\mathbb C(x)\). It is also irreducible in \(\mathbb C[x,y]\): any factor depending only on \(x\) would divide its leading coefficient \(1\), while any other factorization would persist over \(\mathbb C(x)\).

The numbers \(c_1,\ldots,c_9\) are distinct, because cosine is strictly decreasing on \((0,\pi)\). Hence these are nine distinct irreducible quadratic factors, alongside the diagonal factor \(y-x\).

\[

Their only boundary point is \((\infty,\infty)\). This point belongs to the closure of each affine curve, as seen by letting \(u\to\infty\) in
