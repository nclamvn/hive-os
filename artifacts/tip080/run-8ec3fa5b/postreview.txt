1. Recursive interval lemma.

The recursive transformation preserves every letter exactly once, by induction on word length: the disjoint left and right subwords supply their letters, and their common maximum is appended once. Thus the output positions and relative orders in the question are well defined.

For fixed letters a < b, the proof correctly follows the contiguous recursive subword containing both endpoints until its maximum lies in the interval between their positions. While a maximum lies outside that interval, both endpoints and their entire intervening interval remain in the same child. That child is strictly shorter. Consequently this process must terminate, and the first maximum lying within the interval is the maximum of that interval. It cannot be a, since b is also in the interval.

If this maximum is b, the local output is F(L)F(R)b, so a precedes b, regardless of their original orientation. If the maximum is an interior letter, it is greater than b, the endpoints occupy different children, and F(L) is concatenated before F(R). Their relative order is therefore their original left-to-right order. Ancestor concatenations cannot change a relative order already established inside a child output. These cases prove both directions of the claimed equivalence:

  b precedes a in F(w)
  iff b precedes a in w and some letter greater than b lies between them.

In particular, smaller intervening letters do not affect this criterion. The interval reasoning does not rely on the arithmetic checker or on an unproved probabilistic property of F.

2. Uniform relative order and counting.

For a = i and b = i+1, there are exactly k = n-i-1 larger letters. Restricting a uniform permutation to these k+2 distinct letters gives a uniform relative order: each fixed restricted order has the same number of full-permutation extensions. No greater intervening letter occurs exactly when a and b are adjacent in this restriction. Contracting the pair gives k+1 objects; there are (k+1)! orders for each of the two pair orientations. Hence adjacency has probability 2(k+1)!/(k+2)! = 2/(k+2).

On the complementary, nonadjacent event, exchanging the two endpoint labels leaves nonadjacency unchanged and switches their orientation. This is a bijection without fixed points, so exactly half of the nonadjacent orders have b before a. Combining this with the proved interval lemma gives

  P(pos_F(sigma)(i+1) < pos_F(sigma)(i))
    = (1/2)(1-2/(k+2))
    = 1/2 - 1/(n-i+1).

The endpoint case k=0 is covered: all restricted orders are adjacent, and the probability is zero. There is no assumption that different descent events are independent.
