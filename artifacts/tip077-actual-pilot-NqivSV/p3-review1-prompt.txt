## Answer

For every allowed initial segment, at least one of the odd-indexed and even-indexed subsequences is eventually periodic.

## Proof

For integers \(t\ge0\) and \(x,k\ge1\), define
\[
C_t(x)=\#\{i\le t:a_i=x\},
\qquad
H_t(k)=\#\{x\ge1:C_t(x)\ge k\}.
\]
Both quantities are zero when \(t=0\).

We first identify the values occurring infinitely often, then prove a counter lemma that gives periodicity.

### 1. A uniform bound for large values

For \(n\ge N\) and \(k\ge1\),
\[
C_n(k)-C_N(k)=H_{n-1}(k)-H_{N-1}(k).
\tag{1}
\]
Indeed, for each \(i=N,\ldots,n-1\), the recurrence says
\[
a_{i+1}=C_i(a_i).
\]
Thus \(a_{i+1}=k\) precisely when index \(i\) is the \(k\)-th occurrence of its value. The number of such occurrences between indices \(N\) and \(n-1\) is the right-hand side of (1).

Set
\[
K=\max\{N,a_1,\ldots,a_N\}.
\]
For \(k>K\), we have \(C_N(k)=0\) and \(H_{N-1}(k)=0\). Consequently,
\[
C_n(k)=H_{n-1}(k)
\qquad(n\ge N,\ k>K).
\tag{2}
\]
Since \(H_{n-1}(k)\) is nonincreasing in \(k\), so is \(C_n(k)\) on \(k>K\).

We claim that
\[
C_n(k)\le K
\qquad(n\ge N,\ k>K).
\tag{3}
\]
It suffices by monotonicity to prove this for \(k=K+1\). Suppose its count first reaches \(k\) at index \(n\). Then \(n>N\) and
\[
C_{n-1}(k)=k-1.
\]
Monotonicity gives \(C_{n-1}(x)\le k-1\) for every \(x\ge k\). Therefore only the \(k-1\) positive integers below \(k\) could have count at least \(k\), giving
\[
H_{n-1}(k)\le k-1.
\]
But (2) gives \(H_{n-1}(k)=C_n(k)=k\), a contradiction. This proves (3).

### 2. The infinitely recurring values

Let \(R\) be the set of values occurring infinitely often. By (3),
\[
R\subseteq\{1,\ldots,K\}.
\tag{4}
\]
Moreover, \(R\) is nonempty. If only finitely many distinct values occur, at least one must occur infinitely often. If infinitely many distinct values occur, infinitely many first occurrences lie after index \(N\). Every such first occurrence is followed by \(1\), so \(1\) occurs infinitely often.

Write \(r=|R|\), so \(1\le r\le K\). For \(x\notin R\), write \(C_\infty(x)\) for its finite total number of occurrences. There exists an integer \(L\ge K\) such that
\[
C_\infty(x)\le L\qquad(x\notin R).
\tag{5}
\]
Indeed, (3) bounds these totals by \(K\) for \(x>K\), and only finitely many other values remain.

For \(k>L\), (2) and (5) imply
\[
C_n(k)
=\#\{x\in R:C_{n-1}(x)\ge k\}
\qquad(n\ge N).
\tag{6}
\]
For each fixed \(k>L\), all \(r\) counts on the right eventually exceed \(k\). Hence
\[
C_\infty(k)=r\qquad(k>L).
\tag{7}
\]

Now fix \(j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\). Every value \(k>L\) has a \(j\)-th occurrence, by (7). This occurrence lies after \(N\), because \(k>K\) is absent from the initial segment, and its successor is \(j\). Distinct values \(k\) give distinct occurrence positions and hence distinct successor positions. Thus \(j\) occurs infinitely often. We have proved
\[
\{1,\ldots,r\}\subseteq R,
\]
and equality follows because both sets have cardinality \(r\):
\[
R=\{1,\ldots,r\}.
\tag{8}
\]

### 3. Eventual alternation

Choose \(T\ge N+1\) large enough that, for every \(t\ge T\),

* \(a_t\notin\{r+1,\ldots,L\}\);
* \(C_t(i)>L\) for all \(1\le i\le r\).

Such a \(T\) exists by (8).

If \(a_t\le r\), then
\[
a_{t+1}=C_t(a_t)>L.
\]
If \(a_t>L\), then (6) gives \(C_t(a_t)\le r\), while its occurrence at index \(t\) gives \(C_t(a_t)\ge1\). Hence
\[
1\le a_{t+1}\le r.
\]
The sequence therefore eventually alternates between values in \(\{1,\ldots,r\}\) and values greater than \(L\).

Choose \(t_0\ge T\) with \(a_{t_0}\le r\), and put
\[
b_m=a_{t_0+2m},\qquad h_m=a_{t_0+2m+1}
\qquad(m\ge0).
\]
Thus \(b_m\le r\) and \(h_m>L\). Define counters immediately before the occurrence of \(b_m\):
\[
c_i(m)=C_{t_0+2m-1}(i),\qquad 1\le i\le r.
\]
Since the intervening \(h_m\) contributes nothing to these counters,
\[
c_i(m+1)=c_i(m)+\mathbf1_{\{i=b_m\}},
\qquad
h_m=c_{b_m}(m+1).
\tag{9}
\]
Applying (6) at the index containing \(h_m\), and then applying the recurrence, gives
\[
b_{m+1}
=\#\{i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}:c_i(m+1)\ge c_{b_m}(m+1)\}.
\tag{10}
\]
Every \(c_i(m)\) tends to infinity, because every \(i\le r\) occurs infinitely often.

### 4. Counter lemma

**Lemma.** Consider integer counters \(c_1,\ldots,c_r\) and a current index \(b\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\). Each step increments \(c_b\) by one and sets
\[
b'=\#\{i:c'_i\ge c'_b\},
\]
where \(c'\) denotes the updated counters. If every counter tends to infinity, then the index sequence is eventually periodic.

**Proof.** At each stage, let
\[
s_1\ge s_2\ge\cdots\ge s_r
\]
be the counter values sorted in decreasing order, retaining repetitions. Let \(e_i\) be the \(i\)-th unit vector.

An update satisfies
\[
c'=c+e_b,\qquad s'=s+e_{b'}.
\tag{11}
\]
To prove the second equality, put \(v=c_b\) before the update and
\[
q=\#\{i:c_i\ge v+1\}.
\]
The old sorted entry \(s_{q+1}\) equals \(v\). Increasing the chosen counter from \(v\) to \(v+1\) increases precisely this sorted entry by one. The new number of counters at least \(v+1\) is \(q+1\), so \(b'=q+1\). This reasoning includes all ties.

It follows from (11) that the vector
\[
u=c-s+e_b
\tag{12}
\]
is invariant, because
\[
c'-s'+e_{b'}=c-s+e_b.
\]
Set
\[
D=1+\max_i|u_i|.
\]
At every stage,
\[
|c_i-s_i|\le D.
\tag{13}
\]

For \(1\le k<r\), consider the sorted gap
\[
g_k=s_k-s_{k+1}.
\]
If \(g_k>2D\), then every counter labelled \(1,\ldots,k\) strictly exceeds every counter labelled \(k+1,\ldots,r\). Indeed, for \(i\le k<j\), (13) gives
\[
c_i\ge s_k-D>s_{k+1}+D\ge c_j.
\tag{14}
\]

At such a stage, the current index cannot satisfy \(b\le k\). If it did, incrementing that counter would preserve the strict separation between the two labelled groups. Its updated rank count would be at most \(k\), so the next index would again lie in the first group. Inductively, the separation would persist and every subsequent update would remain in that group. All counters in the second group would stay constant, contrary to their assumed divergence. This argument preserves the labelled separation (14); it does not require the sorted gap to remain greater than \(2D\).

Thus, whenever \(g_k>2D\), the chosen counter lies in the lower group. Its old value is at most \(s_{k+1}\). Incrementing it by one cannot raise \(s_k\), because the integer inequality \(s_{k+1}<s_k\) gives
\[
s_{k+1}+1\le s_k.
\]
Nor can any sorted order statistic decrease when one counter is increased. Therefore
\[
g'_k\le g_k\qquad\text{if }g_k>2D.
\tag{15}
\]
For every update, (11) also gives \(g'_k\le g_k+1\). Consequently, at every stage \(m\),
\[
g_k(m)\le G_k:=\max\{g_k(0),2D+1\}.
\tag{16}
\]
Indeed, a gap above \(2D\) cannot increase, and a gap at most \(2D\) can rise only to \(2D+1\).

Summing the gaps bounds the counter range:
\[
\max_i c_i(m)-\min_i c_i(m)
=s_1(m)-s_r(m)
\le B:=\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}G_k.
\tag{17}
\]
For \(r=1\), the sum is empty and the range is zero.

Normalize the counters by defining
\[
d_i(m)=c_i(m)-\min_j c_j(m).
\]
Every \(d_i(m)\) is an integer in \(\{0,\ldots,B\}\). Hence the states
\[
\bigl(b_m,d_1(m),\ldots,d_r(m)\bigr)
\]
belong to a finite set.

The next normalized state is determined entirely by the current state: increment the selected coordinate, compute the next index by the rank rule, and subtract the new minimum. Adding a common constant to all counters affects none of these operations.

Some state therefore repeats. Determinism implies, by induction, that all subsequent states repeat with the same positive period. In particular, the index sequence is eventually periodic. \(\square\)

### 5. Conclusion

Equations (9)–(10) give exactly the process in the lemma, and all its counters tend to infinity. Therefore \((b_m)_{m\ge0}\) is eventually periodic.

Since
\[
b_m=a_{t_0+2m},
\]
this sequence is a tail of the odd-indexed subsequence if \(t_0\) is odd, and a tail of the even-indexed subsequence if \(t_0\) is even. The corresponding parity subsequence is therefore eventually periodic. \(\square\)

## Scope and gaps

The proof applies to every positive integer \(N\) and every allowed positive initial segment, including \(N=1\), \(r=1\), and counter ties. No essential gap remains. The finite-state argument rests on a proved uniform bound, not on simulation.

## Answer

For every allowed initial segment, at least one of the two parity subsequences is eventually periodic.

## Proof

Let
\[
C_t(x)=\#\{j\le t:a_j=x\}.
\]
The recurrence is
\[
a_{t+1}=C_t(a_t)\qquad(t\ge N).
\]

### 1. The infinitely recurring values form an initial interval

Set \(K=\max(a_1,\ldots,a_N)\). We first show that every value greater than \(K\) occurs at most \(K\) times.

Otherwise, let \(t\) be the first index at which any value greater than \(K\) reaches multiplicity \(K+1\). Write the occurrence positions of that value \(x\) as
\[
t_1<\cdots<t_{K+1}=t.
\]
Since \(x>K\), all these positions exceed \(N\). Their predecessors \(y_i=a_{t_i-1}\) satisfy
\[
C_{t_i-1}(y_i)=x>K.
\]
Minimality of \(t\) forces every \(y_i\le K\). Moreover, the \(y_i\) are distinct: the cumulative counts at successive occurrences of a fixed value strictly increase, so that value cannot generate the same successor \(x\) twice. This gives \(K+1\) distinct values in \(\{1,\ldots,K\}\), a contradiction.

Let \(S\) be the set of values occurring infinitely often. Thus
\[
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,K\}.
\]
The sequence is unbounded. Indeed, if its range were finite, some value would occur infinitely often, and its unbounded occurrence counts would appear as subsequent terms, contradicting boundedness. Hence infinitely many distinct values first appear after position \(N\). Each such first appearance is followed by \(1\), so \(1\in S\).

Put \(r=|S|\ge1\). Choose an integer \(L\ge\max(K,N)\) that also bounds the total multiplicity of each finitely occurring value in \(\{1,\ldots,K\}\). The preceding bound then shows that every value outside \(S\) has total multiplicity at most \(L\).

Fix \(x>L\). Every occurrence of \(x\) has a predecessor in \(S\), because no value outside \(S\) can have cumulative count \(x\). Each \(s\in S\) generates \(x\) exactly once, immediately after its \(x\)-th occurrence. That occurrence has index at least \(x>N\), so the recurrence applies. Consequently, every \(x>L\) occurs exactly \(r\) times.

These \(r\) occurrences of \(x\) are followed, in chronological order, by
\[
1,2,\ldots,r.
\]
As there are infinitely many such \(x\), every value in \(\{1,\ldots,r\}\) occurs infinitely often. Therefore
\[
\{1,\ldots,r\}\subseteq S,
\]
and equality follows from \(|S|=r\):
\[
S=\{1,\ldots,r\}. \tag{1}
\]

Choose a time after which all values in \(S\) have cumulative count greater than \(L\). Any later occurrence of a value in \(S\) is followed by a value greater than \(L\). Conversely, every occurrence of a value greater than \(L\) is followed by a value in \(\{1,\ldots,r\}=S\), since its total multiplicity is \(r\).

Starting at a sufficiently late occurrence in \(S\), the sequence therefore alternates between values in \(S\) and values greater than \(L\).

### 2. The small terms satisfy a counter rule

Write the small terms in this alternating tail as
\[
b_1,b_2,\ldots\in\{1,\ldots,r\}.
\]
Let \(c_i(m)\) be the number of occurrences of \(i\) strictly before the position of \(b_m\). Then
\[
c_i(m+1)=c_i(m)+\mathbf1_{\{i=b_m\}}. \tag{2}
\]
The large term between \(b_m\) and \(b_{m+1}\) is
\[
u=c_{b_m}(m)+1=c_{b_m}(m+1)>L.
\]

Each \(i\in S\) generates \(u\) exactly once, immediately after its \(u\)-th occurrence. By the position of the current large term, this generation has happened exactly when
\[
c_i(m+1)\ge u.
\]
Indeed, the current term is large, so every small occurrence counted here lies strictly before it and has already produced its successor. Thus the current multiplicity of \(u\), which determines \(b_{m+1}\), is
\[
b_{m+1}
=\#\{i:c_i(m+1)\ge c_{b_m}(m+1)\}. \tag{3}
\]
Every counter \(c_i(m)\) tends to infinity, because every \(i\in S\) occurs infinitely often.

We prove the needed assertion about this counter process.

### 3. Counter lemma

**Lemma.** Suppose integer counters \(c_1,\ldots,c_r\) and a selected label \(b\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\) evolve as follows: increase \(c_b\) by \(1\), then replace \(b\) by
\[
q=\#\{i:c_i\ge c_b\},
\]
using the updated counters. If every counter tends to infinity, then the sequence of selected labels is eventually periodic.

**Proof.** For \(r=1\), the selected label is always \(1\). Assume \(r\ge2\).

At each state, sort the counters in decreasing order:
\[
d_1\ge d_2\ge\cdots\ge d_r.
\]
For each label \(i\), define
\[
e_i=c_i-d_i+\mathbf1_{\{i=b\}}.
\]
We claim that every \(e_i\) is invariant.

Suppose the selected counter has value \(t\) before its increment. Because the counters are integers,
\[
q=1+\#\{i:c_i>t\},
\]
where the right-hand side uses the old counters. Hence \(d_q=t\). Replacing one counter equal to \(t\) by \(t+1\) changes the sorted vector precisely by increasing its \(q\)-th coordinate by \(1\). This also holds at ties: all preceding coordinates are at least \(t+1\), and all following coordinates are at most \(t\).

Writing primes for the next state, we therefore have
\[
c_i'=c_i+\mathbf1_{\{i=b\}},\qquad
d_i'=d_i+\mathbf1_{\{i=q\}},\qquad b'=q.
\]
It follows that
\[
e_i'
=c_i+\mathbf1_{\{i=b\}}
-d_i-\mathbf1_{\{i=q\}}
+\mathbf1_{\{i=q\}}
=e_i.
\]

Set
\[
E=1+\max_i|e_i|.
\]
At every state,
\[
|c_i-d_i|\le E. \tag{4}
\]

Consider a sorted gap
\[
d_k-d_{k+1}>2E,\qquad 1\le k<r.
\]
For labels \(i\le k<j\), (4) gives
\[
c_i\ge d_k-E>d_{k+1}+E\ge c_j.
\]
Thus the counters with labels \(1,\ldots,k\) strictly exceed all the remaining counters.

The selected label cannot belong to \(\{1,\ldots,k\}\). If it did, its increment would preserve this separation. Its new rank \(q\) would be at most \(k\), so the next selected label would again belong to the same group. Repeating this argument would keep all future increments in that group. The remaining counters would stay constant, contradicting their divergence to infinity.

Therefore, whenever this gap exceeds \(2E\), the selected counter is in the lower group. Its increment cannot increase the gap. To see this precisely, the upper counters remain unchanged. Before the increment every lower counter is at most the minimum upper counter minus \(1\); afterwards it is still at most that minimum. Thus the \(k\)-th sorted counter remains the minimum upper counter, while the \((k+1)\)-st is the maximum lower counter, which cannot decrease.

When the gap is at most \(2E\), one update can increase it by at most \(1\), since exactly one sorted coordinate increases by \(1\). Consequently, with
\[
B=\max\left(
2E+1,\,
\max_{1\le k<r}\bigl(d_k(1)-d_{k+1}(1)\bigr)
\right),
\]
induction shows that every sorted gap is at most \(B\) forever. In particular,
\[
\max_i c_i-\min_i c_i
=d_1-d_r\le(r-1)B. \tag{5}
\]

Normalize the counters by subtracting their minimum:
\[
\widehat c_i=c_i-\min_j c_j.
\]
By (5), the state
\[
(\widehat c_1,\ldots,\widehat c_r,b)
\]
takes values in a finite set. Its transition is deterministic, because adding a common constant to all counters changes neither the comparison defining \(q\) nor the next normalized state.

An infinite trajectory in a finite set repeats a state. Determinism then makes its entire future periodic. In particular, its selected-label sequence is eventually periodic. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

### 4. Return to the original sequence

Equations (2) and (3) give exactly the counter rule in the lemma, and all its counters tend to infinity. Hence
\[
b_1,b_2,\ldots
\]
is eventually periodic.

These small terms occupy every other position of a tail of the original sequence. Their positions have one fixed parity. Therefore the corresponding subsequence—either \((a_{2j-1})_{j\ge1}\) or \((a_{2j})_{j\ge1}\)—is eventually periodic.

## Scope and gaps

The proof applies to every positive integer \(N\) and every positive-integer initial segment. All blocking lemmas are proved above; no essential gap or computational assumption remains.
