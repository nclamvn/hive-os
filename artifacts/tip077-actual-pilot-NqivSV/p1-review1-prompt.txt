## Answer

Exactly the even integers:
\[
\boxed{\alpha\in2\mathbb Z}.
\]

## Proof

For any integer \(m\) and real \(x\),
\[
\lfloor m+x\rfloor=m+\lfloor x\rfloor,
\]
because adding \(m\) preserves the inequalities defining the floor.

Write
\[
\alpha=2q+r,\qquad q=\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\right\rfloor\in\mathbb Z,
\qquad 0\le r<2.
\]
For every positive integer \(n\),
\[
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lfloor k\alpha\rfloor
=2q\sum_{k=1}^{n}k+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lfloor kr\rfloor
=q\,n(n+1)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lfloor kr\rfloor.
\]
Thus \(\alpha\) has the required property if and only if \(r\) does. This reduction holds also for negative \(\alpha\).

We exclude every \(r>0\).

**If \(0<r<1\),** choose
\[
n=\left\lceil\frac1r\right\rceil\ge2.
\]
Since \(n-1<1/r\), every \(1\le k<n\) satisfies \(0<kr<1\). Also, \(1/r\le n<1/r+1\), so
\[
1\le nr<1+r<2.
\]
Therefore
\[
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lfloor kr\rfloor=1,
\]
which is not divisible by \(n\ge2\).

**If \(1\le r<2\),** put \(\beta=2-r\in(0,1]\) and choose
\[
n=\left\lfloor\frac1\beta\right\rfloor+1\ge2.
\]
For \(1\le k<n\), we have \(0<k\beta\le1\). Consequently,
\[
2k-1\le kr=2k-k\beta<2k,
\]
and hence \(\lfloor kr\rfloor=2k-1\).

At \(k=n\), the choice of \(n\) gives
\[
1<n\beta\le1+\beta\le2.
\]
Thus
\[
2n-2\le nr<2n-1,
\qquad \lfloor nr\rfloor=2n-2.
\]
It follows that
\[
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lfloor kr\rfloor
=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(2k-1)-1
=n^2-1.
\]
This is congruent to \(-1\) modulo \(n\), so it is not divisible by \(n\ge2\).

Both cases contradict the required property. Therefore \(r=0\), proving that \(\alpha=2q\) is an even integer.

Conversely, if \(\alpha=2q\) for any \(q\in\mathbb Z\), then
\[
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lfloor k\alpha\rfloor
=\sum_{k=1}^{n}2qk
=q\,n(n+1),
\]
which is divisible by \(n\) for every positive integer \(n\).

## Scope and gaps

The proof covers all real \(\alpha\), including negative values. The inclusive bounds handle all threshold equalities. No essential gaps or computational assumptions remain.

**Answer.** Exactly the even integers:
\[
\boxed{\alpha\in 2\mathbb Z}.
\]

**Proof.** Suppose the divisibility condition holds. For every positive integer \(n\), define
\[
S_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\lfloor k\alpha\rfloor,
\qquad q_n=\frac{S_n}{n}\in\mathbb Z.
\]
For every real \(x\), including negative \(x\), its fractional part
\(\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor\) satisfies \(0\le\{x\}<1\). Thus
\[
q_n=\frac{\alpha(n+1)}2-e_n,
\qquad
e_n=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\{k\alpha\}\in[0,1).
\]

For \(n\ge2\), the difference \(d_n=q_n-q_{n-1}\) is an integer. Moreover,
\[
\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor
=S_n-S_{n-1}
=nq_n-(n-1)q_{n-1}
=nd_n+q_{n-1}.
\]
Substituting \(\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor=n\alpha-\{n\alpha\}\) and
\(q_{n-1}=\alpha n/2-e_{n-1}\) gives
\[
d_n=\frac{\alpha}{2}
+\frac{e_{n-1}-\{n\alpha\}}n.
\]
Because both \(e_{n-1}\) and \(\{n\alpha\}\) lie in \([0,1)\),
\[
\left|d_n-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right|<\frac1n.
\]

For any \(n,m\ge3\), it follows that
\[
|d_n-d_m|
<\frac1n+\frac1m
\le\frac23<1.
\]
Since \(d_n-d_m\) is an integer, it must be zero. Hence there is one integer \(d\) such that \(d_n=d\) for every \(n\ge3\), and consequently
\[
\left|d-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right|<\frac1n
\qquad(n\ge3).
\]
If the left-hand side were some \(\delta>0\), choosing an integer
\(n>\max\{3,1/\delta\}\) would contradict this inequality. Therefore
\(\alpha/2=d\), proving \(\alpha\in2\mathbb Z\).

Conversely, let \(\alpha=2d\) with \(d\in\mathbb Z\). Each \(k\alpha\) is an integer, so for every positive integer \(n\),
\[
\sum_{k=1}^n\lfloor k\alpha\rfloor
=2d\sum_{k=1}^n k
=d\,n(n+1),
\]
which is divisible by \(n\). This calculation applies equally to negative \(d\) and to \(d=0\).

**Scope and gaps.** Both directions hold for all real \(\alpha\), including negative values. There are no remaining gaps.

END ORIGINAL SUBMISSION Y
