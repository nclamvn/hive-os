## Answer

The original attempt is correct. Every essential implication is justified, and it handles eventual constancy and all positive-integer boundary cases. Its classification is indeed \((a,b)=(1,1)\), with \(g=2\).

## Proof (independent audit)

The decisive approach is to check periodicity modulo \(ab+1\). A prime-by-prime analysis would require additional bookkeeping; size bounds alone do not explain why a useful divisor must divide \(g\).

Here is the verification of every essential step.

1. **The modulus and cancellation are valid.** Set \(m=ab+1\). Positivity gives \(m\ge2\). Any common divisor of \(a\) and \(m\) divides \(m-ab=1\), so \(\gcd(a,m)=1\); likewise \(\gcd(b,m)=1\). Bézout’s identity therefore provides inverses of both \(a\) and \(b\) modulo \(m\).

2. **A simultaneous positive period exists.** There are at most \(m^2\) possible ordered residue pairs modulo \(m\). Thus among the \(m^2+1\) pairs indexed by \(0\le j\le m^2\), two coincide, at indices \(r>s\). Consequently,
   \[
   a^r\equiv a^s,\qquad b^r\equiv b^s\pmod m.
   \]
   Cancelling the invertible powers \(a^s,b^s\) gives
   \[
   a^T\equiv b^T\equiv1\pmod m,\qquad T=r-s>0.
   \]
   No assumption about the parity or minimality of \(T\) is needed.

3. **Eventual constancy forces the entire modulus to divide \(g\).** Choose \(k\) with \(kT-1\ge N\). Because \(ab\equiv-1\pmod m\),
   \[
   a(a^{kT-1}+b)\equiv1-1=0\pmod m.
   \]
   Cancelling \(a\) shows \(m\mid a^{kT-1}+b\). The same reasoning gives \(m\mid b^{kT-1}+a\). Hence \(m\) divides their gcd, which equals \(g\). This establishes divisibility by the full composite modulus, including its prime powers.

4. **The next exponent legitimately uses the same \(g\).** Since \(kT\ge N\), both \(a^{kT}+b\) and \(b^{kT}+a\) are divisible by \(g\), and therefore by \(m\). The period congruences yield
   \[
   b\equiv-1,\qquad a\equiv-1\pmod m.
   \]
   Thus \(ab\equiv1\pmod m\). Combining this with \(ab\equiv-1\pmod m\) gives \(m\mid2\). Since \(m\ge2\), necessarily \(m=2\), so \(ab=1\) and positivity forces \(a=b=1\).

5. **Sufficiency holds.** For \(a=b=1\), both expressions equal \(2\) for every positive exponent. Thus \(g=2\) and \(N=1\) work.

## Scope and gaps

There is no essential gap or circular implication. Cases with \(a=1\) or \(b=1\) are covered without exception, and choosing \(k\) sufficiently large handles any positive threshold \(N\).

The argument is uniform in \(a,b\); its finite pigeonhole step proves a general periodicity lemma and does not substitute computational evidence for an unbounded theorem. No correction is required.
