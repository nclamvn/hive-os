## Answer

The only pair is \(\boxed{(a,b)=(1,1)}\). It works with \(g=2\) and \(N=1\).

## Proof

Suppose positive integers \(a,b,g,N\) satisfy
\[
\gcd(a^n+b,b^n+a)=g
\qquad\text{for every integer }n\ge N.
\]
Set \(m=ab+1\). Then \(m\ge2\), and
\[
\gcd(a,m)=\gcd(b,m)=1,
\]
since any common divisor of \(a\) and \(m\), or of \(b\) and \(m\), divides \(m-ab=1\). By Bézout’s identity, both \(a\) and \(b\) have multiplicative inverses modulo \(m\).

There exists a positive integer \(T\) such that
\[
a^T\equiv b^T\equiv1\pmod m.
\]
Indeed, the \(m^2+1\) ordered pairs
\[
(a^j\bmod m,\ b^j\bmod m),\qquad 0\le j\le m^2,
\]
take at most \(m^2\) values. By the pigeonhole principle, two coincide, at indices \(r>s\). Cancelling the invertible powers \(a^s,b^s\) yields the desired congruences with \(T=r-s>0\).

Choose a positive integer \(k\) large enough that \(kT-1\ge N\). Since \(ab\equiv-1\pmod m\),
\[
a(a^{kT-1}+b)=a^{kT}+ab\equiv1-1=0\pmod m.
\]
Cancelling \(a\) gives \(m\mid a^{kT-1}+b\). Similarly,
\[
b(b^{kT-1}+a)\equiv0\pmod m,
\]
so \(m\mid b^{kT-1}+a\). Therefore
\[
m\mid\gcd(a^{kT-1}+b,b^{kT-1}+a)=g.
\]

The next exponent \(kT\) also lies beyond the threshold. Hence \(g\), and consequently \(m\), divides both \(a^{kT}+b\) and \(b^{kT}+a\). Reducing modulo \(m\) gives
\[
b\equiv-1\pmod m,\qquad a\equiv-1\pmod m.
\]
Thus \(ab\equiv1\pmod m\). But \(m=ab+1\) also gives \(ab\equiv-1\pmod m\). It follows that \(m\mid2\). Since \(m\ge2\), we have \(m=2\), so \(ab=1\). Positivity forces \(a=b=1\).

Conversely, when \(a=b=1\),
\[
\gcd(1^n+1,1^n+1)=2
\]
for every positive integer \(n\). This proves sufficiency.

## Scope and gaps

The proof covers all positive integer pairs and every possible threshold \(N\). There are no remaining gaps; no computational evidence is used.
