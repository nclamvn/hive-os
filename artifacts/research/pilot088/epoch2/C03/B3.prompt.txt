PROBLEM
C03
There exists a function \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) satisfying
\[
f\left(\frac{x+2y}{3}\right)\leq\frac{f(x)+2f(y)}{3}\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}\text{ with }x\leq y,
\]
for which there exist \(u,v\in\mathbb{R}\) such that
\[
f\left(\frac{u+2v}{3}\right)>\frac{f(u)+2f(v)}{3}.
\]


REPLAYED RESPONSE 1
**C03. The statement is false: no such function exists.**

We prove that the assumed inequality also holds when \(x>y\). It suffices to show that every function \(g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) satisfying the assumed inequality obeys
\[
g(1/3)\le \frac{2g(0)+g(1)}3.
\tag{1}
\]

Fix an odd positive integer \(N\) divisible by \(3\). Consider a finite random process whose states are
\[
\{-3,-2\}\cup\{i/N:-N\le i\le N\},
\]
starting at \(1/3\). The states \(-3,-2,0,1\) are absorbing. From any other state, make the following transitions:

- From \(i/N\), where \(-N+2\le i\le N-1\) and \(i\ne0\), move to \((i-2)/N\) with probability \(1/3\), and to \((i+1)/N\) with probability \(2/3\).
- From \(-1\), move to \(-3\) with probability \(1/3\), and to \(0\) with probability \(2/3\).
- From \((-N+1)/N\), move to \(-2\) with probability \(1/3\), and to \((-N+3)/(2N)\) with probability \(2/3\).

All destinations belong to the state set because \(N\) is odd. In every transition, if the destinations are \(L<R\), the current state is
\[
\frac{L+2R}{3}.
\]
Consequently, the assumed inequality says that the expected value of \(g\) after a transition is at least its current value. The expected numerical state, meanwhile, is unchanged.

Absorption occurs with probability one. Indeed, repeatedly choosing the destination of probability \(2/3\) strictly increases the state until it reaches \(0\) or \(1\), in at most \(2N+2\) steps. Thus, from every nonabsorbing state, the probability of absorption within that many steps has a positive uniform lower bound.

Let \(T\) be the terminal state, and write
\[
p_0=\Pr(T=0),\qquad p_1=\Pr(T=1),\qquad
q_2=\Pr(T=-2),\qquad q_3=\Pr(T=-3).
\]
The process has finitely many states, so both their numerical values and their \(g\)-values are bounded. Iterating the preceding expectations and passing to the limit therefore gives
\[
g(1/3)\le p_0g(0)+p_1g(1)+q_2g(-2)+q_3g(-3),
\tag{2}
\]
and
\[
\frac13=p_1-2q_2-3q_3.
\tag{3}
\]

We now show that \(q_2,q_3\) tend to zero as \(N\) increases. The only way to enter a negative state from a positive state is the transition
\[
1/N\longrightarrow -1/N.
\]
Once negative, the process cannot become positive; it eventually ends at \(0,-2\), or \(-3\). Starting from \(-1/N\), preservation of the expected numerical state gives
\[
-\frac1N=-2\Pr(T=-2)-3\Pr(T=-3).
\]
Hence the probability of ending at either negative absorbing state, conditional on entering the negative states, is at most \(1/(2N)\). In particular,
\[
q_2+q_3\le\frac1{2N}.
\tag{4}
\]

Equations (3)–(4), together with the sum of the four probabilities being \(1\), imply
\[
p_1\longrightarrow\frac13,\qquad p_0\longrightarrow\frac23,
\qquad q_2,q_3\longrightarrow0.
\]
Taking \(N\to\infty\) through odd multiples of \(3\) in (2) proves (1). This limit involves only the four fixed, finite values \(g(-3),g(-2),g(0),g(1)\); no regularity assumption is needed.

Finally, given \(u>v\), define
\[
g(t)=f\bigl(v+(u-v)t\bigr).
\]
Because this change of variable preserves order, \(g\) satisfies the assumed inequality. Applying (1) yields
\[
f\left(\frac{u+2v}{3}\right)
=g(1/3)
\le\frac{2g(0)+g(1)}3
=\frac{f(u)+2f(v)}3.
\]
For \(u\le v\), this is already the hypothesis. Thus the inequality holds for every ordered pair \(u,v\), making the proposed strict violation impossible. \(\square\)

REPLAYED RESPONSE 2
**C03. The solver’s proof is sound. The proposed existence statement is false.**

The finite-state construction correctly derives the missing inequality without any regularity assumption on \(f\). The following checks cover its potentially delicate points.

- **The transitions are well defined.** An odd positive multiple of \(3\) satisfies \(N\ge3\), and the exceptional destination
  \[
  \frac{-N+3}{2N}
  \]
  is a grid point because \((-N+3)/2\) is an integer. Transition rules apply only to nonabsorbing states.

- **Every transition uses the permitted orientation.** For the ordinary transition, the destinations satisfy \(L<R\), and
  \[
  \frac{(i-2)/N+2(i+1)/N}{3}=\frac{i}{N}.
  \]
  For the two exceptional transitions,
  \[
  \frac{-3+2\cdot0}{3}=-1,\qquad
  \frac{-2+2(-N+3)/(2N)}3=\frac{-N+1}{N}.
  \]
  Thus the numerical state is a martingale, while its \(g\)-value is a submartingale.

- **Absorption and passage to terminal expectations are justified.** Repeatedly choosing the right destination strictly increases the state until absorption. For each fixed \(N\), this gives a uniform positive probability of absorption within a bounded number of steps, hence almost-sure absorption. Both the state and its \(g\)-value are bounded on that finite state space. Bounded convergence therefore proves (2) and (3); no unproved optional-stopping assumption is needed.

- **The estimate on negative terminal mass is valid.** The only transition from a positive state to a negative one is \(1/N\to-1/N\). All transitions from negative states remain nonpositive, including the exceptional transition because \(N\ge3\). Starting at \(-1/N\), the terminal expectation consequently gives
  \[
  2\Pr(T=-2)+3\Pr(T=-3)=\frac1N,
  \]
  so the probability of negative absorption is at most \(1/(2N)\). Conditional on ever entering the negative states, the process restarts at \(-1/N\); the Markov property yields the asserted unconditional bound.

These facts imply
\[
q_2,q_3\to0,\qquad p_1\to\frac13,\qquad p_0\to\frac23.
\]
Although the intermediate \(g\)-values can vary without bound as \(N\) varies, that causes no problem: the final limit involves only the four fixed terminal values. Hence
\[
g(1/3)\le\frac{2g(0)+g(1)}3.
\]

Finally, the affine substitution \(g(t)=f(v+(u-v)t)\) preserves order when \(u>v\), and transfers this inequality exactly to the allegedly violating pair. Pairs with \(u\le v\) are already covered by the hypothesis.
