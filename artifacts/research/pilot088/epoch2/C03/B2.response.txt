**C03. The solver’s proof is sound. The proposed existence statement is false.**

The finite-state construction correctly derives the missing inequality without any regularity assumption on \(f\). The following checks cover its potentially delicate points.

- **The transitions are well defined.** An odd positive multiple of \(3\) satisfies \(N\ge3\), and the exceptional destination
  \[
  \frac{-N+3}{2N}
  \]
  is a grid point because \((-N+3)/2\) is an integer. Transition rules apply only to nonabsorbing states.

- **Every transition uses the permitted orientation.** For the ordinary transition, the destinations satisfy \(L<R\), and
  \[
  \frac{(i-2)/N+2(i+1)/N}{3}=\frac{i}{N}.
  \]
  For the two exceptional transitions,
  \[
  \frac{-3+2\cdot0}{3}=-1,\qquad
  \frac{-2+2(-N+3)/(2N)}3=\frac{-N+1}{N}.
  \]
  Thus the numerical state is a martingale, while its \(g\)-value is a submartingale.

- **Absorption and passage to terminal expectations are justified.** Repeatedly choosing the right destination strictly increases the state until absorption. For each fixed \(N\), this gives a uniform positive probability of absorption within a bounded number of steps, hence almost-sure absorption. Both the state and its \(g\)-value are bounded on that finite state space. Bounded convergence therefore proves (2) and (3); no unproved optional-stopping assumption is needed.

- **The estimate on negative terminal mass is valid.** The only transition from a positive state to a negative one is \(1/N\to-1/N\). All transitions from negative states remain nonpositive, including the exceptional transition because \(N\ge3\). Starting at \(-1/N\), the terminal expectation consequently gives
  \[
  2\Pr(T=-2)+3\Pr(T=-3)=\frac1N,
  \]
  so the probability of negative absorption is at most \(1/(2N)\). Conditional on ever entering the negative states, the process restarts at \(-1/N\); the Markov property yields the asserted unconditional bound.

These facts imply
\[
q_2,q_3\to0,\qquad p_1\to\frac13,\qquad p_0\to\frac23.
\]
Although the intermediate \(g\)-values can vary without bound as \(N\) varies, that causes no problem: the final limit involves only the four fixed terminal values. Hence
\[
g(1/3)\le\frac{2g(0)+g(1)}3.
\]

Finally, the affine substitution \(g(t)=f(v+(u-v)t)\) preserves order when \(u>v\), and transfers this inequality exactly to the allegedly violating pair. Pairs with \(u\le v\) are already covered by the hypothesis.
