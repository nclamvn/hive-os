**C05. The proof is sound; there are no substantive gaps.**

The key steps are valid:

1. Every nonsquare perfect power has a representation \(a^b\) with \(a\ge2\) and \(b\ge3\). If \(a^b\le x\), then \(b\le\lfloor\log_2 x\rfloor\). Counting all such representations may overcount numbers, but this is harmless for the upper bound
   \[
   E(x)\le x^{1/3}\log_2 x.
   \]
   Thus \(E(m^2)=o(m)\).

2. There must be infinitely many open intervals \((m^2,(m+1)^2)\) without perfect powers. Otherwise, each sufficiently late interval would contain a nonsquare perfect power. Because these intervals are disjoint, this would force
   \[
   E(M^2)\ge M-m_0,
   \]
   contradicting \(E(M^2)=o(M)\).

3. For any such empty interval, \(A_n\) is constant on
   \[
   m^2\le n\le (m+1)^2-1.
   \]
   The left endpoint is included correctly: its square is already counted in \(k=A_{m^2}\). The next square lies outside the block.

4. This block has exactly \(2m+1\) integers, whereas
   \[
   k=m+E(m^2)<2m+1
   \]
   for sufficiently large \(m\). Any block of at least \(k\) consecutive integers contains every residue class modulo \(k\), so some \(n\) satisfies \(n\equiv-2024\pmod{k}\). Since \(A_n=k\), the required divisibility follows.

Finally, infinitely many eligible \(m\) remain after discarding finitely many small ones, and their blocks are disjoint. Hence the constructed integers \(n\) are infinitely many and distinct.
