**C05.** Let \(E(x)\) denote the number of nonsquare perfect powers at most \(x\). Any such number has a representation \(a^b\) with \(a\ge2\) and
\[
3\le b\le \lfloor\log_2 x\rfloor.
\]
Consequently, for \(x\ge2\),
\[
E(x)\le \sum_{b=3}^{\lfloor\log_2 x\rfloor}\lfloor x^{1/b}\rfloor
\le x^{1/3}\log_2 x.
\]
In particular,
\[
E(m^2)=O(m^{2/3}\log m)=o(m).
\]
There are exactly \(m\) positive squares at most \(m^2\), including \(1\), so
\[
A_{m^2}=m+E(m^2)<2m+1
\]
for all sufficiently large \(m\).

We claim that infinitely many intervals
\[
(m^2,(m+1)^2)
\]
contain no perfect power. Since these intervals contain no squares, it suffices to exclude nonsquare perfect powers. If the claim were false, there would be an \(m_0\) such that every interval with \(m\ge m_0\) contained a nonsquare perfect power. The disjoint intervals with \(m_0\le m<M\) would then give
\[
E(M^2)\ge M-m_0,
\]
contradicting \(E(M^2)=o(M)\).

Now take any sufficiently large \(m\) whose interval contains no perfect power, and put \(k=A_{m^2}\). Throughout the integer block
\[
m^2,\ m^2+1,\ldots,(m+1)^2-1
\]
we have \(A_n=k\). This block contains \(2m+1\) consecutive integers, and \(k\le2m+1\). Hence it contains an integer \(n\) congruent to \(-2024\pmod{k}\). For that integer,
\[
A_n=k\mid n+2024.
\]

Infinitely many such \(m\) exist, and their integer blocks are disjoint, yielding infinitely many distinct \(n\). \(\square\)
